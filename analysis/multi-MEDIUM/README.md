## Real Process and Queueing Mapping

Vehicles traverse intersections across a 200-intersection Texas road subnetwork heading toward 10 selected boundary exits. Intersections are modeled as bounded FIFO queues with abstract simultaneous server capacity. Road connections are permitted transfers directed strictly toward exits. A vehicle entering at distance $d$ from its nearest exit carries work $W(d+1)$; each queue applies `Sub-1` (1-timestep service), guaranteeing exactly $d+1$ serviced visits before departing at a boundary sink.

This is a synthetic exit-directed traffic model using real road connectivity. It does not model physical lane geometry, traffic signals, turning conflicts, travel time along edges, driver route choice, or calibrated vehicle counts.

## Workload Generation

Poisson arrivals generated by unchanged tool A with preserved job IDs and arrival times. Vehicles are assigned round-robin to the 190 ingress nodes in deterministic BFS order. Work is set by ingress distance to exit ($d+1$). Demands are calibrated against the network's nominal bottleneck arrival capacity (~18.1 arrivals/step):

All actual counts are strictly within the required MEDIUM assignment range (10,000–100,000 external jobs). Three seeds (42, 43, 44) are evaluated for every demand and resource level.

Replication across seeds 42, 43, and 44 demonstrates tight statistical stability:

- Stressed −10%: 314, 312, 348 drops (mean 325 $\pm$ 20).
- Stressed −20%: 859, 857, 900 drops (mean 872 $\pm$ 24).

- Overloaded baseline: 1,679, 1,716, 1,754 drops (loss rate 8.59% $\pm$ 0.14%).

## Comparison with SMALL Scale

1. **Exit Diversity**: SMALL had only 1 exit, creating an inescapable single bottleneck where all traffic funneled. MEDIUM has 10 distributed boundary exits, distributing departures and allowing higher aggregate throughput (peak 38–57 vs 4–8 in SMALL).
2. **Path Length & System Latency**: The maximum exit distance expanded from 8 to 14 hops. Mean transit latency increased from ~5.0 steps in SMALL to ~7.3 steps in MEDIUM.
3. **Resilience to Overload**: Under overloaded demand, SMALL dropped 31.3% of vehicles because the single exit backed up completely. In MEDIUM, the 10 exits absorb load in parallel, keeping overloaded loss down to 8.6%.

## USE Metrics

Per-queue USE metrics (Utilization, Saturation, Errors) were computed across 10, 100, and 1000-step rolling windows:
- **Boundary Exits (e.g. q181)**: High sustained utilization throughout the arrival window, but lower saturation than in SMALL because departures are shared across 10 exit points.
- **Interior Junctions (e.g. q164)**: Show transient saturation spikes during stressed and overloaded runs as confluent traffic streams merge.
- **Peripherals (e.g. q5)**: Low utilization, minimal queuing, and zero errors; serve primarily as originating sources.
- **C Measurement Notes**: C's `Utilization` is binary active/idle indicator, `Saturation` is rounded mean waiting count, and `Errors` are window event counts.

## Throughput and Latency

Network throughput is measured as completed vehicle departures per timestep across all 10 sinks. Peak network throughput reaches 38 vehicles/step at baseline, scaling up to 57 vehicles/step under +100% server capacity. 

End-to-end latency reflects external arrival to final sink departure:
- Under low demand, latency concentrates tightly around hop distance (mean 7.30 steps).
- Under stressed baseline, mean latency increases modestly to 8.23 steps (mean queue wait < 1 step).
- When servers are reduced by 30%, mean latency doubles to 15.56 steps as queues build up at lower-capacity nodes.

## Does the Behavior Make Sense?

Yes:
1. **Parallel Exits Alleviate Concurrency**: Having 10 boundary exits prevents a total gridlock and reduces loss rate compared to the 1-exit SMALL topology.
2. **Headroom Eliminates Loss**: Adding +25% servers eliminates all losses at stressed demand, bringing latency down to the theoretical routing minimum.
3. **Heterogeneous Server Reduction Impact**: Reducing servers by 10%–30% removes servers in BFS order, selectively reducing capacity at interior merge nodes. This creates localized queue overflows, explaining the jump in drops from 1 to 969.

## Practical Takeaways (Within Model Limits)

1. **Exit Capacity Scaling**: Multi-exit networks significantly outperform single-exit networks by distributing termination bottlenecks across the boundary.
2. **Buffer Headroom**: In networks near nominal capacity, modest capacity buffer (+25%) absorbs stochastic arrival bursts and prevents cascading queue overflows.
3. **Targeted Reductions**: Cutting resources uniformly or by graph-order rounds creates sharp localized chokepoints. Infrastructure capacity must align with confluent flow volume.
