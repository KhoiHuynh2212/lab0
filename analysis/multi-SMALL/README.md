Exit-directed synthetic traffic on a 20-node sample of SNAP roadNet-TX. See [common README](../multi-common/README.md) for model, validation and workflow.

## Real Process and Queueing Mapping

Vehicles traverse intersections in a sampled Texas road subnetwork, heading toward a boundary exit. Each intersection is a bounded FIFO queue with abstract server capacity. Roads are transfer links. A vehicle entering at distance `d` from the exit carries work `W(d+1)`; each queue applies `Sub-1` (1 timestep service), so the vehicle visits exactly `d+1` queues before completing at the exit.

This is a synthetic model of exit-directed traffic on real road connectivity. It does not reproduce actual Texas traffic volumes, signal timing, travel distances, or driver behavior.

## Workload Generation

Poisson arrivals generated by A with preserved IDs and times. Vehicles are assigned round-robin to the 19 ingress nodes in BFS order. Work is set by ingress distance to exit. Three demand levels calibrated against the network's no-loss bottleneck capacity:

| Demand | Lambda | Horizon | Nominal pressure | Actual jobs (seed 42) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Low | 2.0 | 1,000 | 0.50 | 2,074 |
| Stressed | 4.0 | 500 | 1.00 | 2,067 |
| Overloaded | 5.99 | 334 | 1.50 | 2,019 |

All actual counts are within the required SMALL range (1,000–10,000). Three seeds (42/43/44) per demand level.

## Experiment Matrix

27 runs: 9 demand baselines (3 demands × 3 seeds) + 18 resource variants (6 server changes × 3 seeds at stressed demand). Resource variants use identical workload bytes.

## Results Summary

### Demand Baselines (seed 42, 80 servers)

| Demand | Jobs | Completed | Dropped | Loss % | Timesteps | Mean Response |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Low | 2,074 | 2,074 | 0 | 0.0 | 1,008 | 5.760 |
| Stressed | 2,067 | 2,013 | 54 | 2.6 | 514 | 9.723 |
| Overloaded | 2,019 | 1,387 | 632 | 31.3 | 352 | 15.176 |

Mean response here is the exact average of final departure minus external arrival over completed vehicles, measured in steps. Each completed vehicle has equal weight; dropped vehicles are excluded. It is not an average of C's rounded per-departure-timestep averages. Tables round to three decimal places; the CSV retains the computed value. Runs with no completions have an undefined mean rather than zero.

Low demand clears all vehicles with minimal waiting (mean response 5.760 steps ≈ average serviced path length). Stressed demand approaches capacity and begins dropping at congested queues. Overloaded demand loses nearly a third of vehicles.

### Resource Sensitivity (stressed demand, seed 42)

| Server Δ | Total | Completed | Dropped | Loss % | Mean Response |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| +100% | 160 | 2,067 | 0 | 0.0 | 5.743 |
| +50% | 120 | 2,067 | 0 | 0.0 | 5.801 |
| +25% | 100 | 2,067 | 0 | 0.0 | 5.961 |
| Baseline | 80 | 2,013 | 54 | 2.6 | 9.723 |
| −10% | 72 | 1,740 | 327 | 15.8 | 11.340 |
| −20% | 64 | 1,547 | 520 | 25.2 | 18.040 |
| −30% | 56 | 1,582 | 485 | 23.5 | 24.200 |

**Key observations:**
- **+25% eliminates all losses** at stressed demand. The system transitions sharply from lossy to lossless near the capacity boundary.
- **Reductions cause disproportionate damage**: −10% (removing 8 servers) causes 15.8% loss, while −30% (removing 24 servers) causes 23.5%. The relationship is nonlinear because heterogeneous allocation concentrates bottlenecks at low-ranked BFS-order nodes.
- **−30% drops fewer than −20%** for seed 42 (485 vs 520). This is a routing effect: with fewer servers, more vehicles are rejected earlier in their path, reducing downstream congestion. Different seeds show similar patterns (seed 43: 377 vs 400; seed 44: 423 vs 443).
- **Mean response time climbs steeply** from 9.723 steps at baseline to 24.200 at −30%, reflecting greater waiting among completed vehicles. Comparisons also reflect which vehicles survive rejection.

### Consistency Across Seeds

All three seeds at each demand/resource level show consistent behavior. Standard deviations across seeds for loss rate at stressed baseline: ±1.2 percentage points. The model's deterministic BFS-order ingress assignment and round-robin routing produce reproducible congestion patterns.

## USE Metrics

Per-queue USE (Utilization, Saturation, Errors) computed at 10/100/1000-step windows:
- **Exit q15**: highest utilization at all demand levels; saturates first under stress.
- **Interior q10**: high utilization due to flow concentration; queues build under stressed/overloaded.
- **Peripheral q18**: low utilization at distance 8; only processes vehicles entering at that endpoint.
- C's utilization is rounded 0/1 activity, not a server-normalized percentage. Saturation is rounded mean waiting occupancy. Error windows are cumulative difference counts.

## Throughput and Latency

System throughput measured as departures per timestep at the exit sink. Peak throughput is 4 (the exit's server count) at all demand levels, confirming the exit as the bottleneck. Under low demand, departures are sparse; under overloaded demand, the exit runs at capacity continuously during the arrival window.

End-to-end latency (external arrival to final sink departure) among completed vehicles increases from 5.760 steps at low demand to 15.176 at overloaded. The minimum possible latency equals the ingress distance +1 (one service step per queue). Additional latency is waiting time at congested queues.

## Does the Behavior Make Sense?

Yes, within model assumptions:
1. **Single exit creates a natural bottleneck** — all traffic funnels through q15, which limits network throughput to its server capacity (4/step).
2. **Path-length variation** — vehicles entering at distance 8 consume 9 service slots vs 2 for distance-1 ingresses. Longer paths create more intermediate congestion.
3. **Loss occurs at congested interior nodes**, not just the exit — when a queue's waiting capacity (20) is full, arriving vehicles are dropped immediately.
4. **Resource additions beyond +25% show diminishing returns** because the exit capacity is the binding constraint. Adding servers to non-bottleneck nodes doesn't help.
5. **Low demand is well below capacity** (λ=2 vs bottleneck capacity ~4), so zero loss and minimal waiting are expected.

## Practical Takeaways (Within Model Limits)

1. **Single-exit networks are fragile.** One bottleneck determines the entire system's maximum throughput regardless of interior capacity.
2. **Moderate overcapacity (+25%) eliminates losses** at the capacity boundary. In practice, planning for peak demand requires modest headroom.
3. **Cutting resources has nonlinear effects.** A 10% reduction causes 16% loss because congestion cascades through connected queues.
4. **Heterogeneous allocation matters.** BFS-order server removal concentrates reductions at graph-peripheral nodes, creating localized bottlenecks even when total capacity seems adequate.
5. **These are synthetic model conclusions.** Real traffic requires calibrated demand, multi-destination routing, time-varying arrival rates, and physical capacity constraints not represented here.

## Limitations

- Single exit at SMALL scale makes the bottleneck obvious and limits resource sensitivity insights.
- Round-robin ingress assignment creates spatial correlations in arrival patterns.
- All routes have the same number of visits from a given entry; no route-choice effects.
- C's rounded USE metrics limit precision for utilization comparisons across server counts.
- 20 queues is the minimum for "tens"; behavior may differ at larger network sizes.
