## Real Process and Queueing Mapping

Vehicles navigate intersections across a 2,000-intersection Texas road subnetwork heading toward 62 distributed boundary exit sinks. Intersections are modeled as bounded FIFO queues with abstract simultaneous server capacity. Road connections are permitted transfers directed strictly toward exits. A vehicle entering at distance $d$ from its nearest exit carries work $W(d+1)$; each queue applies `Sub-1` (1-timestep service), guaranteeing exactly $d+1$ serviced visits before departing at a boundary sink.

This is a synthetic exit-directed traffic model using real road connectivity. It does not model physical lane geometry, traffic signals, turning conflicts, travel time along edges, driver route choice, or calibrated vehicle counts.

## Workload Generation

Poisson arrivals generated by unchanged tool A with preserved job IDs and arrival times. Vehicles are assigned round-robin to the 1,938 ingress nodes in deterministic BFS order. Work is set by ingress distance to exit ($d+1$). Demand is calibrated against network capacity ($\lambda = 19.37$ arrivals/step over 10,323 timesteps):

| Demand | Lambda ($\lambda$) | Horizon | Nominal Pressure | Actual Jobs (Seed 42) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| **Stressed** | 19.37 | 10,323 | 1.00 | 200,372 |

The workload of **200,372 vehicles** strictly satisfies the assignment requirement for the LARGE scale study (100,000–1,000,000 jobs across 1,000s of queues).

## Experiment Matrix and Sensitivity Study

The study evaluates the full 7-configuration resource sensitivity matrix specified in the assignment on the 200,372-vehicle Stressed workload:
- **1 Baseline configuration**: 8,000 servers (+0%).
- **3 Capacity expansion variants**: +25% (10,000 servers), +50% (12,000 servers), +100% (16,000 servers).
- **3 Capacity reduction variants**: −10% (7,200 servers), −20% (6,400 servers), −30% (5,600 servers).

All 7 runs use byte-identical workload files, ensuring strict, controlled paired evaluations.

### Analysis of System Behavior

1. **Monotonic Latency and Loss Scaling**: As server capacity increases from −30% to +100%, vehicle drop count falls from 31,577 down to 9,070 (a **71.3% reduction**), and loss rate drops from 15.76% down to 4.53%.
2. **Topological Hop-Distance Asymptote**: Mean response time decreases with additional capacity but remains above the average shortest-path distance. Under +100% capacity, mean response is 19.11 steps, which includes approximately 1.04 steps of residual waiting per completed vehicle. The network's average hop distance provides a lower bound, but queue contention at interior merge nodes prevents reaching it even at double capacity.
3. **Congestion Wait Collapse**: Total vehicle waiting car-steps collapse by **76.8%** from 910,190 down to 211,299 under +100% servers. Conversely, under −30% servers, waiting time nearly doubles to 1,760,661 (+93.4%), proving that severe congestion develops at interior merge nodes when capacity is constrained.

|---|---:|---:|---:|

| **Baseline Mean Response** | 9.72 | 8.23 | 22.04 |
| **Peak Throughput** | 4 | 38 | 49 |

### Key Cross-Scale Takeaways:
1. **Network Diameter and Inherent Latency**: Mean latency at stressed baseline is 9.72 steps for SMALL (20 queues), 8.23 for MEDIUM (200 queues), and 22.04 for LARGE (2,000 queues). SMALL's higher baseline reflects its single-exit bottleneck rather than network diameter alone. In LARGE, vehicles generate over 3.42 million queue visits across longer paths.
2. **Boundary Concurrency vs Aggregation Pressure**: Having 62 boundary sinks in LARGE allows sustained aggregate throughput (peak 49 vehicles/step). However, because 1,938 ingress flows merge into shared intermediate arterial queues before reaching the boundary, interior merge nodes become localized bottlenecks, resulting in a baseline drop rate of 13.11% that requires +100% server capacity to reduce to 4.53%.
3. **Computational Resource Scaling**: Simulation and validation scale superlinearly with visit count. At 3.42 million visits, memory management becomes the primary engineering constraint rather than raw CPU compute.

## Memory and Hardware Profiling

Hardware and memory profiling were measured during execution on host hardware (Intel/Windows, 16 GB RAM):
- **Simulator (C++ Objective E)**: Completed 200,372 vehicles across 2,000 queues in **112–156 seconds**, utilizing ~120 MB RAM. Highly efficient event-driven engine.
- **Validation (Python)**: A child Python process (accounting and validation) peaked at **6.51 GB** working set. The parent Python process (orchestration and analysis) peaked at **2.26 GB**. The summed process-tree working set reached **8.64 GB**. Shared pages may be counted more than once across processes; the per-process peaks are OS high-water values.
- **Analysis and Plotting (Objective C)**: C++ accessory tools (`derive-event-metrics`, `derive-sys-metrics`) executed in 100–220 seconds over the 3.4M-row event logs.

## USE Metrics

USE metrics (Utilization, Saturation, Errors) across rolling windows (10, 100, 1000 timesteps) demonstrate distinct structural behavior:
- **Boundary Exit Sinks (e.g. q1913)**: Near-continuous utilization during peak influx, but low saturation due to rapid departure absorption across the 62 exit sinks.
- **Interior Merge Junctions (e.g. q1797)**: Experience severe saturation spikes and queue drop errors under −10% to −30% resource constraints as converging traffic branches exceed server capacity.
- **Peripheral Nodes (e.g. q5)**: Minimal utilization and zero queue buildup; function strictly as origin points.

## Practical Takeaways (Within Model Limits)

1. **Topology Dictates Minimum Latency**: In large distributed networks, extra capacity yields diminishing latency returns once mean response approaches the average hop distance. Under +100% capacity, mean latency drops to 19.11 steps but still includes residual waiting; further gains require topological changes, not just more servers.
2. **Buffer Headroom Prevents Drop Cascades**: At 2,000 nodes, capacity buffers (+50% to +100%) cut dropped vehicles by up to 71% and suppress congestion buildup at interior confluence junctions.
3. **Interior Arterial Bottlenecks**: In contrast to smaller networks where boundary exits are the main chokepoints, massive networks suffer bottlenecks primarily at intermediate convergence points where multiple transit corridors merge.
